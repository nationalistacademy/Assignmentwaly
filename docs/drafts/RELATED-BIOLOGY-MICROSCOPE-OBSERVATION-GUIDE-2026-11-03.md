<article style="max-width:900px;margin:0 auto;padding:8px;line-height:1.75;font-family:Arial,sans-serif;overflow-wrap:anywhere;">
<p>A microscope helps learners examine structures that are too small to see clearly with the unaided eye. Good microscopy is not just about making an image larger: the learner must focus carefully, handle slides properly, record what is actually visible and avoid adding details that were not observed. This is a general study guide. Follow your laboratory's instructions and use prepared slides only under appropriate supervision.</p>

<h2>1. Know the main microscope parts</h2>
<p>A standard school light microscope may include an eyepiece, objective lenses, revolving nosepiece, stage, stage clips or mechanical holder, light source, condenser, diaphragm, coarse-focus control and fine-focus control. Designs vary, so identify the parts on the instrument in front of you rather than assuming every model is identical.</p>

<h2>2. A careful focusing routine</h2>
<ol>
<li>Place the microscope on a stable, clean surface and carry it with two hands when moving it.</li>
<li>Check that the lowest-power objective is selected before placing a prepared slide.</li>
<li>Secure the slide gently and position the area of interest over the light opening.</li>
<li>Use the light and diaphragm controls as instructed to obtain suitable illumination.</li>
<li>Look through the eyepiece and bring the image into focus using the method approved for that microscope.</li>
<li>Move to a higher-power objective only when the image is centred and the supervisor has shown you the correct procedure.</li>
<li>Use fine focusing as required; do not force the lens or focus controls against the slide.</li>
</ol>
<p>Some microscopes have different focusing arrangements. Follow the instrument manual and instructor's demonstration. Never force a control or allow an objective lens to strike the slide.</p>

<h2>3. Make observations before drawing</h2>
<p>Begin by writing a few factual observations: the shape, relative size, arrangement and visible boundaries of the structures. Separate what you can actually see from what you think the structure might be. If the image is unclear, say so and ask for help instead of guessing.</p>

<h2>4. How to make a scientific diagram</h2>
<ul>
<li>Use a clear title that identifies the specimen or prepared slide as provided by the teacher.</li>
<li>Draw only structures that are visible and relevant to the task.</li>
<li>Use clean, continuous lines and avoid decorative shading unless specifically required.</li>
<li>Keep label lines straight and make sure they point to the intended structure.</li>
<li>Do not label a feature simply because it appears in an online diagram; confirm it in your own specimen or the assigned reference.</li>
<li>If the drawing is not to scale, do not imply that it is.</li>
</ul>

<h2>5. Magnification and field of view</h2>
<p>Total magnification in a conventional compound light microscope is commonly calculated by multiplying eyepiece magnification by objective magnification. For example, a 10× eyepiece used with a 4× objective gives 40× total magnification. This is a calculation of magnification, not a guarantee that every detail will become clearer. As magnification increases, the visible field generally becomes smaller and focusing becomes more demanding.</p>

<h2>6. Slide handling and hygiene</h2>
<p>Use only prepared or instructor-approved slides. Handle glass carefully, keep the work area tidy, and report broken slides immediately. Do not prepare biological samples, stain specimens or culture microorganisms outside a properly equipped and supervised teaching laboratory. Follow the laboratory's rules for gloves, handwashing, cleaning and disposal.</p>

<h2>7. Frequent mistakes</h2>
<ul>
<li>Starting with a high-power objective before locating the specimen.</li>
<li>Moving the slide without watching how the image changes.</li>
<li>Using labels that cannot be matched to visible structures.</li>
<li>Drawing details from memory rather than observation.</li>
<li>Touching lenses with fingers or using unsuitable materials to clean them.</li>
<li>Handling biological material without permission or supervision.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Self-assessment</h2>
<ol>
<li>Why is it useful to begin with a low-power objective?</li>
<li>How is total magnification calculated on a conventional compound microscope?</li>
<li>Why should labels on a biological diagram match observed structures?</li>
<li>What should a learner do if a slide is broken or a specimen is unclear?</li>
<li>Why is magnification different from image resolution?</li>
</ol>
