<p>A clear practical record helps a learner explain what was done, what was observed and what the evidence means. It should report real observations—not invented readings or copied results. This guide explains a general record-writing method. Always follow the exact format and safety instructions given by your teacher or laboratory supervisor.</p>

<h2>1. What belongs in a practical record?</h2>
<p>A practical record is a structured account of a supervised activity. Depending on the task, it may contain:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Title:</strong> a short name that identifies the activity.</li>
<li><strong>Objective:</strong> what the activity is intended to demonstrate or measure.</li>
<li><strong>Apparatus and materials:</strong> only the items actually supplied and used.</li>
<li><strong>Safety notes:</strong> relevant hazards and precautions explained by the supervisor.</li>
<li><strong>Method:</strong> a concise account of the approved procedure in the learner's own words.</li>
<li><strong>Observations:</strong> readings, visible changes or other evidence recorded at the time.</li>
<li><strong>Calculations:</strong> formula, substitution, working and units, where applicable.</li>
<li><strong>Result and conclusion:</strong> what the observations support, including limitations.</li>
</ul>

<h2>2. Observation is not the same as conclusion</h2>
<p>An observation is information recorded from what was seen or measured. A conclusion is the interpretation drawn from those observations. For example, “the liquid changed from clear to pale pink” is an observation; explaining what that change indicates in a particular supervised test is interpretation. Keep the two separate so another reader can follow your reasoning.</p>

<h2>3. Record measurements with units</h2>
<p>A number without a unit can be ambiguous. Write the quantity and its unit together, use the precision supported by the instrument, and do not add extra decimal places to make a result look more accurate. If a table is required, give each column a clear heading and unit.</p>

<h2>4. Show calculations clearly</h2>
<p>When a calculation is part of an assigned activity, write the formula first, define the symbols, substitute the recorded values, show the arithmetic and include the final unit. Check whether the formula's assumptions apply to the actual reaction or measurement. Do not apply a shortcut formula merely because it appears in an unrelated example.</p>

<h2>5. Write a useful conclusion</h2>
<p>A conclusion should answer the objective and refer to the recorded evidence. Avoid unsupported statements such as “the experiment was 100% accurate.” If the result may have been affected by reading uncertainty, equipment limits or procedural variation, state that carefully.</p>

<h2>6. Laboratory safety comes first</h2>
<p>Do not perform chemical tests, prepare solutions, heat substances or mix materials at home based on an online article. Use only an authorised laboratory, the approved procedure, appropriate protective equipment and direct supervision. Never taste chemicals, directly smell unknown substances, or pour materials into a drain unless the supervisor's disposal instructions specifically permit it.</p>

<h2>7. Common practical-record mistakes</h2>
<ul>
<li>Writing readings from memory instead of recording them during the activity.</li>
<li>Leaving units out of tables and calculations.</li>
<li>Mixing observations with explanations.</li>
<li>Claiming an activity was performed when it was not.</li>
<li>Copying another student's data or using invented results.</li>
<li>Ignoring the teacher's prescribed record format.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Quick self-check</h2>
<ol>
<li>Can another reader identify the objective?</li>
<li>Are all recorded values genuine and labelled with units?</li>
<li>Are observations separate from conclusions?</li>
<li>Can the calculation be followed line by line?</li>
<li>Have safety and disposal instructions been followed?</li>
</ol>
