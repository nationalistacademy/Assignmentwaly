## 3. Course 258 — Lab Techniques in Chemistry

### Q1. Basic chemistry laboratory apparatus

**Keep:** beaker, flask, test tube, graduated/measuring cylinder, pipette, burette, watch glass, spatula, stand/clamp and burner. This is useful introductory content.

**Correct:**
- Correct the Urdu rendering of “Beaker” to **بیکر**; the current “بیوٹر” is misleading and can be confused with burette.
- A beaker is mainly for holding, mixing and heating liquids; its graduations are approximate, not accurate volumetric measurement.
- A graduated cylinder measures volume more accurately than a beaker. A volumetric pipette transfers a specified accurate volume; a burette delivers a measured variable volume, commonly in titration.
- Avoid suggesting all flasks are used identically; distinguish conical/Erlenmeyer and round-bottom flasks where relevant.
- Remove the corrupted table fragments in the alternate page (for example “三个方面” and stray dashes).

**Disposition:** Retain and rewrite the apparatus table with a “main use / measurement accuracy” distinction.

### Q2. Measuring instruments

**Keep:** analytical balance, pH meter, thermometer, hydrometer and viscometer.

**Correct/qualify:**
- “0.0001 g” is a possible analytical-balance readability, not a universal specification. Say “depending on the instrument model; check its stated readability and calibration.”
- A pH meter measures pH. Do not define its universal range as exactly 0–14; instrument/electrode range and sample conditions matter, and concentrated solutions can fall outside the simplified 0–14 classroom range.
- “−10°C to 110°C” is not a universal laboratory thermometer range. State that range depends on the thermometer.
- Avoid presenting mercury thermometers as a recommended default; mention safer digital/alcohol alternatives and follow the laboratory's equipment policy.
- Keep hydrometer = liquid density/specific gravity and viscometer = viscosity.

**Disposition:** Retain with instrument-specific range caveats and remove fixed universal ranges.

### Q3. Filtration

**Keep:** definition; filtrate/residue terminology; simple filtration using filter paper and funnel; vacuum filtration using a Büchner funnel/flask and vacuum source.

**Correct:**
- Correct spelling to “Filtration”.
- Vacuum filtration is primarily used to speed separation and collect a solid, often with reduced residual liquid; do not claim it is specifically for “very fine particles”. Filter pore size and material determine what particles are retained.
- Avoid a universal claim that simple filtration is only for large particles.

**Disposition:** Retain the two-method comparison and add a clear note that the filter medium determines separation.

### Q4. Distillation

**Keep:** vaporisation followed by condensation; simple vs fractional distillation; salt water as a simple-distillation example; petroleum fractions as a fractional-distillation example.

**Correct:**
- Remove fixed boiling-point-difference cut-offs such as “50°C or more” and “25°C or less”; suitability depends on mixture behaviour and apparatus, not those universal thresholds.
- Explain that simple distillation can separate a volatile solvent from a non-volatile solute or liquids with sufficiently different boiling points.
- Fractional distillation uses a fractionating column and repeated vaporisation/condensation to improve separation of volatile components with closer boiling points.
- Do not state that every petroleum product is separated by a simple two-liquid process; crude oil is a complex mixture.

**Disposition:** Retain with a corrected comparison table and no arbitrary temperature cut-offs.

### Q5. Titration

**Keep:** titrant, analyte, burette, indicator, endpoint and quantitative concentration analysis.

**Critical correction:**
- The current formula **C₁V₁ = C₂V₂** is valid only for a 1:1 reaction stoichiometry (with compatible concentration units). It is not a universal titration equation.
- The general calculation must use the balanced reaction's mole ratio: determine moles from concentration × volume, then apply stoichiometric coefficients to solve for the unknown.
- The endpoint is the observed indicator change; it is intended to approximate the equivalence point, not necessarily identical to it.
- Use “concentration” rather than vague “strength” where quantitative chemistry is intended.

**Disposition:** Retain the explanation and procedure conceptually, replace the universal formula claim with a worked 1:1 example plus a clear stoichiometry warning. Any laboratory execution must follow the course instructor's approved procedure.

### Q6. Chemical identification: colour tests, flame tests and pH

**Keep:** iodine/starch, Biuret/protein, Fehling's/reducing sugar, flame-test colours, litmus/universal indicator/pH meter.

**Correct:**
- Iodine + starch: blue-black; Biuret reagent + proteins/peptide bonds: violet/purple; Fehling's reagent gives a brick-red precipitate with reducing sugars under the specified heated test conditions. Do not say all sugars give the same result.
- Flame colours: sodium—intense yellow; potassium—lilac (can be masked by sodium contamination); calcium—orange-red/brick-red; barium—apple-green; copper—blue-green. Standardise colour names and note that flame tests are qualitative and can be affected by contamination.
- The alternate page's instruction to use a platinum wire is not a safe do-it-yourself instruction. Present flame tests only as supervised laboratory demonstrations under the official practical manual.
- pH paper/indicators give approximate or colour-based readings; a calibrated pH meter gives an instrumental measurement.

**Disposition:** Retain as a comparison chart with qualitative-test limitations and supervised-lab warning.

### Q7. General preparation of a chemical compound (alternate URL only)

**Keep:** objective, balanced equation, stoichiometric calculation, apparatus preparation, isolation, purification and characterisation as high-level concepts.

**Correct:**
- This is too generic to be a safe “procedure for any compound”. Different reactions have different hazards and conditions.
- Add risk assessment, SDS review, approved course protocol, appropriate PPE, supervision and waste disposal.
- “Check expiry date” is not a universal substitute for checking reagent identity, purity, storage and SDS.
- Characterisation methods such as melting point or spectroscopy apply only where suitable.

**Disposition:** Preserve as a conceptual workflow, not a practical recipe. Do not publish step-by-step synthesis instructions without the actual course experiment and instructor-approved safety controls.

### Q8. Chemistry laboratory safety (alternate URL only)

**Keep:** lab coat, appropriate eye protection, closed shoes, tied hair, labelled containers, no eating/drinking, report accidents, use intact apparatus.

**Correct:**
- Replace “rubber gloves” with “chemical-resistant gloves selected for the specific chemical and task”; glove material is not universal.
- Do not advise smelling chemicals unless a specific approved wafting procedure is taught and authorised. Never smell directly.
- Replace “do not pour chemicals into the sink” with “dispose of each chemical according to the laboratory's approved waste procedure; never assume sink disposal is allowed.”
- Keep fire extinguisher/emergency equipment location and instructor reporting.
- Do not imply a towel is a general substitute for heat-resistant tongs/gloves.

**Disposition:** Retain and strengthen; make it clear that this is not permission to perform experiments at home.

### Course 258 short questions

Retain the short-question format, but correct the beaker spelling/use, qualify the filtration answer, qualify the titration formula (1:1 only), and state that acid turns blue litmus red (not simply “litmus paper turns red” without naming the starting colour). Keep the closed-shoes safety answer.

## 4. Course 259 — Lab Techniques in Biology

### Q1. Microscope parts and care

**Keep:** eyepiece, objective lenses, stage, clips, condenser, diaphragm, light source, coarse/fine adjustment and base; lens-paper cleaning, dust cover, two-hand carrying and avoiding lens collision.

**Correct:** distinguish optical parts from mechanical parts; do not invent a fixed number of “main parts”. Explain that magnification is the product of eyepiece and objective magnifications if the course book covers it. Do not touch optical glass with fingers or use ordinary tissue.

**Disposition:** Retain with a labelled diagram only if the image is original/licensed and the labels are checked.

### Q2. Slide preparation and staining

**Keep:** clean slide, specimen, stain where appropriate, cover slip, removal of excess stain, and purpose of improving contrast/visibility.

**Correct:**
- Staining can improve contrast and reveal structures; do not claim ordinary stains generally distinguish living from dead cells. That requires specific viability stains/methods.
- Different stains have different targets and protocols. Methylene blue, iodine and Gram stain are not interchangeable.
- Keep the cover-slip technique only as a general classroom method and refer to the exact course practical manual.

**Disposition:** Retain a conceptual outline; label the stain as specimen-dependent.

### Q3. Onion cells vs cheek/eyelid cells

**Content mismatch:** the question wording refers to “eyelid cells” in one extraction while the answer describes **cheek cells**. This must be made consistent.

**Keep:** plant vs animal cell comparison; cell wall; typical shape; visible nucleus; specimen observation.

**Correct:**
- If the intended specimen is cheek epithelium, rename the question to “cheek cells” and use a properly sourced cheek-cell description. Do not silently claim cheek cells are eyelid cells.
- Onion epidermal cells have a cell wall and often a regular rectangular arrangement; cheek epithelial cells lack a cell wall and are more irregular.
- Do not say onion bulb epidermal cells always contain chloroplasts; the ordinary onion bulb epidermis typically does not show chloroplasts.
- Keep the cell-type comparison simple and avoid claiming every specimen looks identical.

**Disposition:** Retain after resolving the specimen wording and correcting the chloroplast statement.

### Q4. Sterilisation methods

**High-priority scientific correction.**
- Autoclaving is a moist-heat sterilisation method, but **121°C / 15 psi / 15–20 minutes is not a universal cycle for every load**. Correct parameters depend on the validated equipment cycle, load, packaging and material.
- Dry heat can sterilise suitable materials under validated time/temperature conditions; do not present one time range as universal.
- A 0.22 µm membrane can remove many bacteria from compatible liquids, but filtration is not a universal sterilisation method for every organism or virus.
- **70% alcohol is a disinfectant, not a sterilant**: alcohols do not destroy bacterial spores reliably. Do not list alcohol as a general sterilisation method.
- UV is not a universal sterilisation method; efficacy depends on wavelength, dose, exposure, distance, shadows and surface access.

**Disposition:** Rewrite the title/definition distinction between sterilisation and disinfection; move alcohol to the disinfection subsection. Cite CDC guidance. Do not publish as a practical operating protocol.

### Q5. Bacterial culture and culture media

**Keep:** culture definition; Nutrient Agar, MacConkey Agar, Blood Agar and Potato Dextrose Agar as media examples.

**Critical correction:**
- Nutrient agar is a general-purpose medium for many non-fastidious bacteria.
- MacConkey agar is selective/differential: it inhibits many Gram-positive organisms and helps differentiate lactose fermenters among Gram-negative bacteria.
- Blood agar is enriched and can demonstrate haemolysis.
- **Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) is primarily used for fungi/yeasts/moulds, not as a routine bacterial culture medium.** Do not group it as an ordinary bacterial medium without explaining this distinction.
- Do not include home culture instructions or incubation recipes. Culturing unknown microorganisms can create a biological hazard.

**Disposition:** Retain the media comparison with organism/use distinctions and a supervised-laboratory-only notice.

### Q6. Catalase and amylase tests

**Keep:** catalase as an enzyme that breaks down hydrogen peroxide and amylase as an enzyme that breaks down starch; the conceptual positive/negative observations.

**Correct/safety:**
- Do not provide an unsupervised procedure for culturing or testing unknown bacteria.
- Use prepared teaching material/approved organisms only under the instructor's biosafety procedure.
- Human saliva is a biological specimen; do not invite students to handle it casually. Use instructor-approved prepared amylase material and safe disposal.
- Explain that bubbles in a catalase test indicate oxygen release from hydrogen peroxide decomposition, but the result must be interpreted with controls and the prescribed method.

**Disposition:** Retain as principle, purpose and interpretation; remove any implication that students should run the test at home.

### Q7. Plant cross-sections and stomata (alternate URL only)

**Keep:** plant tissue cross-section concept; stomata, guard cells and gas exchange/transpiration.

**Correct:**
- Use “transpiration / بخارات کے اخراج” rather than the literal “sweating” wording in Urdu.
- Any cross-section cutting or slide preparation must be described only as a supervised practical from the course manual.
- The nail-polish imprint method should not be called the only or universal method; identify it as one possible teaching demonstration if authorised.

**Disposition:** Retain as a labelled concept diagram and observation guide after course-manual confirmation.

### Q8. Biology laboratory safety (alternate URL only)

**Keep:** personal protection, hand hygiene, no eating/drinking, clean work area, label samples, safe disposal, report spills/incidents.

**Correct:** add specimen-specific biosafety, approved disinfectant/contact time, sharps/glass disposal and handwashing. Never instruct students to culture unknown environmental samples outside a supervised laboratory.

**Disposition:** Retain and strengthen.

### Practice page Q1. Computer definition and types

**Keep:** input → processing → output → storage; supercomputer, mainframe, PC, laptop and tablet examples.

**Correct:** distinguish computer classes by scale/capability (supercomputer/mainframe) from form factors (desktop/laptop/tablet). Laptop and tablet are not parallel categories to supercomputer/mainframe. Use “computer system” rather than an over-literal “electronic machine” if appropriate.

### Q2. Hardware and software

**Keep:** physical/tangible hardware vs instructions/software; input, output, processing and storage examples; system software vs application software.

**Correct:** CPU is a processing component, not the only processing device; drivers and utilities can be classified under system software. Keep examples current and do not imply all applications are Microsoft products.

### Q3. CPU, RAM, ROM and hard disk

**Keep:** CPU role; RAM as volatile working memory; non-volatile storage; HDD vs SSD.

**Correct:**
- “RAM speed is measured in GHz” is wrong in the current text. GHz normally describes CPU clock frequency; modern memory transfer rates are commonly expressed in MT/s (depending on memory type/specification).
- RAM capacity is measured in bytes (often GB); retain.
- ROM is not always literally read-only in modern systems. Firmware is stored in non-volatile memory, often flash; avoid “cannot be written” as an absolute.
- BIOS/UEFI firmware is stored in non-volatile flash memory on modern systems, not necessarily a fixed read-only ROM chip.
- HDD/SSD are secondary storage devices; retain the non-volatile distinction.
- Avoid saying the CPU makes all “decisions”; explain instruction execution and processing.

### Q4. Internet history, advantages and disadvantages

**Keep:** definition, communication, education, business, access to information, privacy and security concerns.

**Correct dates:**
- ARPANET development began in the late 1960s; do not call it simply a 1960s Department of Defense “project” without context.
- TCP/IP development occurred during the 1970s; ARPANET standardised on/adopted TCP/IP in January 1983.
- Tim Berners-Lee proposed the Web in 1989 and developed the first browser/server in 1990; do not say he invented the Web only in 1990.
- Distinguish the Internet (network infrastructure) from the World Wide Web (one service/system that uses it).

### Q5. WWW, browsers and search engines

**Keep:** Internet vs WWW distinction; browser and search engine definitions; examples such as Chrome, Firefox, Edge, Safari, Google and Bing.

**Correct:**
- The current browser/search-engine tables are malformed (stray “}” and inconsistent columns); rebuild them.
- A browser is software for accessing/rendering web content; a search engine is a service for finding indexed content. They are not interchangeable.
- Remove unsupported marketing descriptions such as “fastest” or claims that a browser's built-in VPN guarantees privacy. Product features change; keep neutral examples.

### Q6. Email and email etiquette

**Keep:** clear subject, greeting, concise message, proofreading, appropriate closing, attachments and careful Reply All use.

**Correct:** account-creation steps change with provider UI, age and verification rules. Present them as general steps and link to the provider's current official help. Do not tell students to publish or share passwords, date of birth or phone details in examples. Use placeholder email addresses only.

### Q8. Cybersecurity and cybercrime

**Keep:** phishing, malware, identity theft, unauthorised access, social engineering, cyberstalking and data exposure; updates, multi-factor authentication and caution with suspicious links.
