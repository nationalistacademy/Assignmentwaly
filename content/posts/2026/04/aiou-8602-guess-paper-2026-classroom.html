📖 12 Exam-Ready Questions (Click to Reveal Answers)

1 Define classroom assessment. Critically discuss the key principles of effective classroom assessment. +
Answer: Classroom assessment is gathering, interpreting, and using information about student learning. Key principles: Validity (measures what it intends), Reliability (consistent results), Fairness (unbiased), Practicality (feasible). Example for validity: A math test shouldn't require high reading skills. Balancing these principles is the art of teaching.
2 Explain the role of assessment in teaching and learning process. +
Answer: Assessment serves 4 key functions: (1) Diagnosing – pre-tests identify prior knowledge; (2) Monitoring – formative quizzes guide instruction; (3) Improving instruction – results tell teachers what worked; (4) Evaluating achievement – summative tests assign grades. Assessment FOR learning is more powerful than assessment OF learning.
3 Define educational objectives. Compare original vs revised Bloom's Taxonomy. +
Answer: Educational objectives state what students should be able to do. Original Bloom's (1956): Knowledge, Comprehension, Application, Analysis, Synthesis, Evaluation. Revised (2001): Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyze, Evaluate, Create. Key changes: verbs instead of nouns, "Create" as highest order. Importance: ensures tests assess higher-order thinking, not just recall.
4 What is test reliability? Explain three types and factors affecting it. +
Answer: Reliability = consistency of test scores. Three types: Test-Retest (same test twice), Inter-Rater (different scorers agree), Internal Consistency (items measure same construct). Factors affecting reliability: test length (longer = more reliable), item difficulty (too easy/hard reduces spread), scoring objectivity, student conditions (fatigue).
5 Define validity and describe its types with measurement methods. +
Answer: Validity = test measures what it claims. Types: Content validity (expert review using TOS), Criterion-related (correlation with external measure), Construct validity (factor analysis). A test can be reliable but not valid. Example: A reliable watch always shows 7:00 – it's consistent but invalid for telling actual time.
6 Rules for writing high-quality MCQs with examples. +
Answer: Rules: (1) Clear stem presenting one problem; (2) Plausible distractors; (3) Avoid "all/none of above"; (4) Grammatically consistent options; (5) Randomize correct answer position; (6) 3-5 options. Example: Good MCQ – "What is the capital of France?" with distractors Berlin, Madrid, Rome. Poor – "France is a country and its capital is..." with unrelated options.
7 What is Table of Specification (TOS)? Explain its preparation and significance. +
Answer: TOS is a two-way chart mapping content vs cognitive levels. Steps: (1) List topics; (2) Count instructional hours; (3) Calculate percentages; (4) Distribute items; (5) Allocate across Bloom's levels. Significance: Ensures content validity – test accurately samples the domain. Prevents over-testing easy topics.
8 Differentiate objective vs subjective tests with advantages/disadvantages. +
Answer: Objective tests (MCQs, true/false) – machine-scorable, high reliability, but encourage recognition. Subjective tests (essays) – assess higher-order thinking, but time-consuming to score. Best practice: mix both. Objective for breadth, subjective for depth.
9 Explain NRT vs CRT – purposes, advantages, disadvantages. +
Answer: Norm-Referenced (NRT) compares students to peers (percentile ranks). Used for selection (e.g., university entrance). Criterion-Referenced (CRT) measures mastery of objectives (pass/fail). Example: Driver's license test = CRT (score 80% to pass). University entrance = NRT (top 10% get admission).
10 Differentiate aptitude vs achievement tests. +
Answer: Aptitude tests measure potential/future ability (SAT, IQ tests). Achievement tests measure past/present learning (final exam, unit test). Aptitude predicts performance; achievement documents learning.
11 Role of interview and observation as assessment techniques. +
Answer: Interviews (structured, semi-structured, unstructured) allow probing, clarify understanding, assess oral communication. Limitations: time-consuming, bias, anxiety. Observations (participant/non-participant, structured/unstructured) capture real behavior, minimal disruption. Limitations: observer bias, Hawthorne effect (behavior changes when watched).
12 Role of parent-teacher conferences and key considerations. +
Answer: Parent-teacher conferences share progress, discuss development, build partnerships. Key considerations: prepare evidence, start positive, be specific, listen actively, develop action plan, follow up. Effective conferences transform parents into active partners.
